Today I decided to start writing about some of the interesting things I encounter while improving my development skills in the AI era.

Part of the inspiration came from Daniel Bourke and his idea of **learning in public**: build things, share what you learn, and let the learning process itself become part of your work.

The funny thing is that, looking back, I realised I was doing something very similar almost 20 years ago.

## It started with games

My development journey started on **15 July 2006**, when I registered on [Web-Tourist.net](https://web-tourist.net/), a Bulgarian community where people were learning web development and game development.

My plan was simple.

I was into World of Warcraft, and I wanted to make games.

At the same time, browser-based games such as **Хановете** and **Bulfleet** were popular in Bulgaria. They weren't real-time graphical games like World of Warcraft. They were browser-based strategy games driven largely by server-side data: resources, buildings, armies, technologies, timers, rankings, and interactions between players.

And I thought:

*If I spend enough time learning this stuff, maybe I can build something like that.*

So I started learning web development.

![Screenshot of Web-Tourist.net, the Bulgarian web development community where I started learning](/images/web-tourist.png)

## Apparently, I was already learning in public

Only a year later, in 2007, I had already published five tutorials on Web-Tourist.

[Some of them are still preserved by the Internet Archive.](https://web.archive.org/web/20071013153554/http://web-tourist.net/login/login/newsearch.php?profil=Malfurion)

The code wasn't good.

There was basically no security, little or no form validation, no edge-case handling, PHP mixed directly with HTML, and not nearly enough explanation.

But I was **14 years old**.

To me, being able to write a script that actually did something useful was awesome.

And when I learned something, I wanted to share it.

So I did.

I built and published things like a platform for sharing lessons, a small game where people shared links to grow their virtual cities — more clicks meant a bigger city — and a file-upload system that allowed users to attach text to uploaded files.

They were small projects, but they worked.

And I kept learning.

## The first paid website

In 2009, I got my first paid development job.

The funny part is that the website is still online today, with almost the same desktop-era layout:

[https://www.keytex-bg.com/](https://www.keytex-bg.com/)

Behind it was a small custom CMS that allowed the owner to edit page content.

With my first real website online, I felt ready to become a web developer.

I did a few freelance websites, which eventually led to my first full-time development job at **Creative Web**.

I made plenty of mistakes there.

Thankfully, I had a very patient manager who gave me the opportunity to learn from them.

I also got my first taste of mentoring. At one point, I was helping a junior front-end developer learn JavaScript and CSS while she was on maternity leave.

I still remember cutting pieces of paper and moving them around on a desk to demonstrate how a web slider would behave if it existed in the physical world.

## From web development to iOS

At Creative Web, one of our clients wanted an iOS application for a summer promotion. I got involved in the project, and that sparked my interest in iOS development.

Eventually, in 2013, it led me to **MacroPinch**, where I managed to get hired as an iOS developer despite having very little professional iOS experience.

Once again, I had someone willing to take a chance on me.

There I worked on several applications, including a new version of **Cardiograph**, which used the phone's camera to measure a person's heart rate.

I also worked on **Outwalk**, a social step-tracking application.

Outwalk became especially important for me because my responsibilities went far beyond writing an iOS client.

I built the backend that stored the data, connected users, and handled notifications. I worked on the iOS app. Because I was responsible for the backend and much of the product architecture, I ended up coordinating the Android development as well. I also brought in a friend from Web-Tourist to work on the UX, which made the project even more enjoyable.

He is still an iOS developer today, which is pretty cool.

## Finally making games

Around 2017, the mobile-app business was changing. Advertising revenue was becoming less attractive, and the company started putting more resources into its game studio, **NoSxFive**.

I was offered the opportunity to join.

And that meant learning game development.

This was interesting because, despite having spent more than a decade as a developer by then, I had surprisingly little actual game-development experience.

I had made two or three tiny Flash games around 2008 and one small browser-based clicker game.

That was pretty much it.

But making games had been the reason I started programming in the first place.

So, more than ten years after registering on Web-Tourist because I wanted to learn how to build games, I finally got the opportunity to do it professionally.

And once again, the company gave me the time to learn game development properly.

Since then, I've worked on six games as part of NoSxFive, two of which I developed largely on my own, and I've continued supporting the studio's other games as well.

## Then came machine learning

During the pandemic in 2020 — before ChatGPT made AI part of everyday conversation — I became increasingly interested in machine learning and AI.

I started reading and experimenting in my spare time.

Then life happened, other priorities took over, and most of those hobby projects were put aside.

Until 2025.

That year, I got the chance to work on something else I had always found interesting: software used in medicine.

I worked on **VNG To Go**, a mobile application for recording eye movements, including nystagmus, together with the position and movement of the head.

Part of that work involved eye detection.

At roughly the same time, I was also thinking about whether machine learning could help me create smarter bots for **Candy Champions**.

Those two problems pulled me back toward ML.

And this time I stayed.

## Back where I started

So here I am.

I've now spent a little more than a year seriously learning machine learning alongside my main work in game development.

And somewhere along the way I realised something amusing.

In 2007, when I was 14, I learned something, built a small project, wrote about it, and shared it with other developers.

Almost twenty years later, my plan is basically the same.

The tools are different.

The problems are harder.

Hopefully the code is better.

But the idea hasn't changed much.

**Learn something. Build something. Share what you learned.**

That's what this blog will be about.

I'll write about the parts of software development, game development, system design, machine learning and AI that I find interesting while working through them.

Some posts will be things I've figured out.

Others will probably be things I'm still figuring out.

That's the point.

I'm learning in public again.

Follow along.
